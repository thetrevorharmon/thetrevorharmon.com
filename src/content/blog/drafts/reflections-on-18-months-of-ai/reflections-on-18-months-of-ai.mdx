The past 18 months of my career has been a distinct time. AI has evolved from ChatGPT–which at the time was functionally only a step above Google–to a technology that has pervaded seemingly every corner of my life. I've been up and down about this change, feeling excited and energized about it one minute, and then insecure and worried the next. While I don't have everything “figured out”, I am feeling more secure about how to use it in my life and specifically in my career. I've gone from asking questions like “will AI replace my job next year?” to teaching homesites at work about how to leverage AI more effectively on the job.

## Reflexive usage

In the summer of 2024, I heard [Tobi Lütke](https://x.com/tobi?lang=en)'s annual Shopify Summit talk. At first, it was standard fare–but halfway through he revealed that _he_ didn't write his talk but that he had a team of AI agents write it for him[^tobi-talk-recap]. At the time, even the concept of AI agents was totally novel, let alone outsourcing a company summit talk to a team of them. It was so early days that Tobi had to buy servers and write custom software to try out the idea.

Later in April 2025, he released an internal memo, where he declared that "reflexive AI usage is now a baseline expectation at Shopify"[^reflexive-ai-memo]. This memo was unsurprising considering he had been very vocal about AI and our need to take it seriously; the messaging simply codified the growing understanding and culture of AI use at Shopify. I would sum up the understanding I've had over time from his leadership as: 

> AI is an exciting, powerful technology that is not optional to stay relevant as an individual or a company in 2026.

## learning to ride

- over the next year I began investing heavily in learning AI
- I felt like I was riding the wave; at times I was pumped about what it could do and how fast certain tasks went
- other times I was worried about if it would replace me
- I found it was useful to apply a tool from cognitive behavior therapy called cognitive restructuring; the idea of challenging your thoughts (in this case, fears) and restructuring them as more accurate statements
- Often my fears were rooted in the unknown or the assumed, but I learned that I could reframe them acuratley, with the overall sentiment being "I solve problems, and problems will exist regardless of how AI is used"
- Related to overcoming the fear related to AI was learning to disregard the hype. I found that the hype in either direction wasn't very useful, as it would cause me to overly-focus on it
- at a certain point I decided I shouldn't focus on how things _used_ to be before AI in my career, but instead just accept what is and learn to thrive on it. As silly as it sounds to quote a value of the company I work, it was learning to thrive on change

The last year and a half of my career has been the most up and down year so far. The introduction of AI has been really interesting to go through because it has constantly made me question my career and the long-term nature of my career and what that will look like in one to three to five to ten years. I think there have been two pieces of direction I have received from various leaders that have really shaped the way that I think about AI.
The first is from my work leader, Toby Lutke, who is the CEO of Shopify. He has been an early adopter of AI and did a lot of interesting AI stuff that was very very cutting edge before it became mainstream, and he talked about it in his summit talk in 2024. In March 2025, there was an internal memo that was then leaked that he posted on Twitter, where he describes that there's an expectation at Shopify that AI use is reflexive, that the people at Shopify should learn how to use AI in a deep way that will impact their work and accelerate their ability. I had already set a goal at work to learn how to use AI better. So the timing of the memo for me was fortuitous because I had spent a few months trying to wrap my brain around what an LLM can do and how I can incorporate it into my workflow. It also sort of supercharged the internal culture and conversation around AI at work, and lots of people at Shopify have been early adopters to the latest and greatest AI tooling, and so I've been able to have easy access to some of the thought leaders in that way.


The second piece of direction is from a spiritual leader, David A. Bednar, who is a member of the Quorum of the Twelve Apostles and a leader of my church. He gave a devotional address called Things As They Really Are 2.0 in November 2024. That was the first time and really the most direct time I have heard a spiritual leader of my church talk about AI and how to think about AI and how to use AI. He cautioned about the moral perils that come with the technology that AI enables. He also talked about how to use AI, and one of the guiding principles was this idea that God created us to be creators, not consumers. There is meaningful spiritual work needed that AI cannot supplant. The takeaway for me was that I should look at AI as a tool that can accelerate my thinking, kind of like "the computer is a bicycle for the mind" quote by Steve Jobs. But I shouldn't use AI to replace me as a creator.


These aren't truly opposing viewpoints—both leaders value AI adoption. The tension is more subtle: Bednar emphasizes retaining ownership of creation, while Lutke emphasizes using AI to create new things. It's a difference in emphasis between two leaders whose guidance I value, and that tension keeps me balanced. AI is called a probabilistic tool where it takes an input and then tries to determine the most probable output and then outputs that next. Typical programming languages or software historically has been deterministic, where if you give it something, it does some kind of computation and returns the same thing every time, which is a deterministic state. Being able to think about AI and its probabilistic nature as a tool instead of a replacement has been very useful because it's given me opportunities to identify how I can accelerate my work.


The journey to where I am today with AI has been very up and down. There have been periods of time where I was learning and growing and discovering new things, and it was exciting. And there have been periods of time where I was struggling and I couldn't seem to figure it out or I was feeling like I was going to be replaced by AI because of what I was seeing. I think part of this truly is just learning to address fear in general and think through fear through exercises like cognitive behavioral therapy teaches us. Those kinds of exercises are helpful when being paralyzed or inhibited by fear. I also try not to listen to the hype about AI in either direction because it's a very new technology and it's apparent that many people are still learning how to use it and what we should be using it for and things like that. Over time I have felt like AI will not replace me, but learning AI and being able to adopt it as a tool that I'm effective with is critical to staying relevant in the future. I try not to put any energy towards worrying about what used to be and simply accept what is and try to learn how to thrive in the reality of today.

When ChatGPT first came out, I was asking very basic questions, and slowly as tooling has gotten better, I have tried to adopt tools along the way and figure out ways I can use those tools to accelerate the thinking that I'm already doing. These days I spend a lot of time with Anthropic's Claude Code as it is a very strong accelerator for writing code, which is one of my primary duties. I tend to spend a lot more time thinking about problems and thinking about solutions to those problems and I'm creating the solution in a more high level theoretical way and then relying on the technology to implement the solution, and then I go back and check the work and make sure it's correct.

The productivity gains have been dramatic in certain areas—tasks like writing tests for my code now take 20% of the time they used to. I can ask AI to generate tests, then evaluate their quality and sufficiency. However, my work has also become more fractured. I often run multiple Claude Code sessions concurrently, working on different pieces simultaneously rather than sequentially. This shift has coincided with my growth from senior to staff developer, where I'm deliberately taking on work with wider-ranging impact.

I have found that there are various dangers of using AI and one of those is what I would call the slop loop, where I start to try to get the LLM to do something over and over and I can't be successful with it because I haven't really taken the time to think about the problem and understand the solution, and I'm essentially outsourcing my thinking instead of outsourcing the execution of my thinking. For example, when Claude Code doesn't correctly implement a feature or fix a bug, I've caught myself repeatedly prompting it for better output rather than stepping back to evaluate whether my initial inputs were clear or whether I've actually thought through the solution deeply enough. Each iteration produces more "slop," and I end up stuck in an unproductive loop.

There is a guy at Shopify named Ronie Uliana who I had an interesting conversation with, and he described using AI as someone who sews clothing discovering a sewing machine, how it's like someone who has been doing something by hand for a long time has discovered how to use a machine, and that greatly accelerates how they're doing it. If you think about the skill of sewing by hand compared to the skill of using a sewing machine, they are really two different skills that have an overlap in the kind of output. That has really shaped my thinking because I have tried to adopt AI and have felt similar things of the feeling of writing code by hand feels very slow and cumbersome now compared to using an AI tool to do a lot of the driving of the writing of the code, and then I come in, provide feedback, review it, make sure that it's ready for production and ready to power Shopify.

I maintain creative ownership by doing the thinking about problem-solving myself. I'll use AI to analyze a problem or explore options, but I own the thought process and the final solution. For problems that have been solved many times before—where AI's probabilistic nature can reliably generate good solutions—I lean on it heavily. But at Shopify's scale and complexity, I often face intersections of problems that haven't been solved before, and that's where I must do the deeper thinking and understanding myself. There are certain things that I do by hand or without any AI generating anything for me, like when I am doing things of a spiritual nature, and I try to maintain that same sense of ownership and creativity in my professional work as well.
